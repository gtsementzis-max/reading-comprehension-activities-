Life in the ocean

The ocean is the largest habitat on Earth, and it is home to millions of living things. Scientists divide the ocean into layers based on how much sunlight reaches the water. Near the surface, the water is bright and warm, so tiny plants called phytoplankton can grow there. These plants are the beginning of the food chain. They feed small fish, which are later eaten by larger animals such as tuna and sharks. As you travel deeper, the sunlight fades and the water turns cold and dark. Very few plants can survive this far down. Even so, the deep ocean is far from empty. Strange and surprising creatures have found ways to live where the sun never shines.

To survive in the ocean, animals have developed special features called adaptations. In the sunlit waters near the top, many fish use camouflage to stay safe. Their silvery sides reflect light, which makes them hard for predators to see. Other animals, like the octopus, can change the color of their skin in seconds to blend into rocks and coral. In the deep, dark ocean, some animals make their own light through a process called bioluminescence. The anglerfish, for example, dangles a glowing lure in front of its mouth to attract prey in the blackness. Each of these adaptations helps an animal find food, hide from danger, or survive in a world that can be cold, dark, and full of hunters.

Words to watch: habitat, phytoplankton, adaptation, camouflage, bioluminescence, predator, prey, lure.

Reading level

The passage is written at roughly a grade-5 level: varied sentence length, a clear main-idea-and-details structure, and target vocabulary introduced with context clues so the meaning can be inferred from surrounding words.

Step 2 · comprehension answers

1-B main idea   2-C key detail   3-B vocabulary in context   4-C inference   5-B cause and effect   6-C inference / main idea

Questions 4 and 6 are the inference questions — the answers are not stated word-for-word. If she gets the recall questions (2, 5) but misses 4 and 6, she is reading accurately but not yet connecting ideas across sentences, which is the next skill to coach.

Step 3 · matching answers

phytoplankton → C   adaptation → E   camouflage → A   bioluminescence → F   predator → B   lure → D

After she finishes, ask how she figured out a hard one — "what words in the sentence told you?" Naming the clue ("it said 'make their own light', so bioluminescence must mean making light") turns a lucky guess into a transferable strategy.

Step 4 · fill-in-the-blank answers

1. phytoplankton   2. adaptation   3. camouflage   4. bioluminescence   5. predator   6. prey   7. habitat   8. lure

Sentences 5 and 6 (predator / prey) are the pair to watch — they describe the same hunting relationship from opposite sides. Mixing them up is worth a quick talk about which animal is doing the hunting versus being hunted.

Extension activities (project capstone)

  • Draw a favorite ocean animal and label one adaptation it uses to survive.
  • Pick one real deep-sea creature, find two facts about it, and write them in her own words.
  • Write her own sentence for two or three of the vocabulary words — the hardest level, because there is no word bank to lean on.
  • Discuss: "Which adaptation do you think is the most useful, and why?"
